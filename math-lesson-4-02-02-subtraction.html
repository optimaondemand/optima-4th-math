Today's Mission
Use the same chart as yesterday, but reverse the move. Stack two numbers, subtract column by column from the ones — and when a column doesn't have enough, regroup from the next place left.
❓
Essential Question
If carrying gives 10 of a smaller place when a column overflows, what's the move when a column doesn't have enough?

Yesterday you carried when a column reached 10. Today you do the opposite. When a column needs to subtract more than it has, you regroup — take 1 from the next place left and turn it into 10 of the current place.

Same chart. Same place value. The trade just runs the other direction: 1 hundred = 10 tens. 1 ten = 10 ones. Always.

📐 What You Just Saw

The standard algorithm for subtraction.

Step 1 — Stack with the bigger number (the minuend) on top. Ones under ones. Same alignment as addition.

Step 2 — Subtract each column from the ones moving left.

Step 3 — Regroup when a top digit is smaller than the bottom digit. Take 1 from the place to the LEFT (which becomes one less) and add 10 to the current place (which becomes ten more). Now subtract.

That's the whole procedure. Regrouping is just place value's rule running in reverse — 1 of any place equals 10 of the place to its right.

🔗 The Algorithm, Column by Column

Here's every column of 52,846 − 18,259. Each row shows the column subtraction, whether you regroup, and what gets written.

Column Subtract Regroup? Write?
Ones 6 − 9 → need more Borrow 1 from tens. 16 − 9 = 7
Tens 3 − 5 → need more Borrow 1 from hundreds. 13 − 5 = 8
Hundreds 7 − 2 = 5 No regroup. Write 5
Thousands 2 − 8 → need more Borrow 1 from ten-thousands. 12 − 8 = 4
Ten-thousands 4 − 1 = 3 No regroup. Write 3. Difference: 34,587

The procedure never changes. Every column: check if the top digit is enough. If yes, just subtract. If no, regroup from the place to its left — and then subtract. Regrouping is place value's way of saying "we'll trade 1 of the bigger place for 10 of the smaller place. Same total value."

Worked Example: How Many More Fans?
Draw a bar model. Estimate. Then subtract by place.
Problem

The home opener drew 72,415 fans. The next home game drew 48,728 fans. How many MORE fans came to the opener? Use a bar model first, then estimate, then subtract exactly.

Watch the Math Crew solve it together:

H
Hazel — draws the bar model
"Two bars side by side, longest on top. Top bar is 72,415 (opener). Bottom bar is 48,728 (next game). The GAP between them is the difference — the answer."
F
Felix — estimates
"Round each to the nearest 1,000. 72,415 → 72,000. 48,728 → 49,000. 72,000 − 49,000 = 23,000. So the difference should be about 23,000."
Hazel's Bar Model — Comparison
Opener: 72,415
Next game: 48,728
Difference: ?
Find the GAP — the part of the opener's bar that sticks out past the next game's.

72,415 − 48,728 = 23,687

A
Atlas — subtracts by place
"Stack with 72,415 on top. Ones 5 − 8 → regroup from tens. Tens 1→0, ones 5→15. 15 − 8 = 7. Tens 0 − 2 → regroup from hundreds. Hundreds 4→3, tens 0→10. 10 − 2 = 8. Hundreds 3 − 7 → regroup from thousands. Thousands 2→1, hundreds 3→13. 13 − 7 = 6. Thousands 1 − 8 → regroup from ten-thousands. Ten-thousands 7→6, thousands 1→11. 11 − 8 = 3. Ten-thousands 6 − 4 = 2. Difference: 23,687."
C
Cora — checks by adding back
"Add the difference back to the smaller number: 23,687 + 48,728 = 72,415. That matches the opener total — so 23,687 is right. The estimate (23,000) is right next to it, too."
💡

The whole routine, applied to subtraction: Hazel's bar model showed it was a COMPARE problem (find the gap). Felix's estimate set the target. Atlas's algorithm did the work column by column with four regroupings. Cora verified by ADDING the answer back — when difference + smaller = bigger, the subtraction is right.

Algorithm vs Count-Up
Two ways to find 8,032 − 5,847. Same answer, different strategy.

Two students subtracted the same two numbers. One used the standard algorithm. The other counted UP from the smaller number to the larger one.

STUDENT A — Standard Algorithm
"Stack and subtract from ones.
Ones 2 − 7 → regroup. Tens 3→2, ones 12. 12−7=5.
Tens 2 − 4 → regroup. Hundreds 0→… 0 is empty, so go further. Thousands 8→7, hundreds 10. Then hundreds 10→9, tens 12. 12−4=8.
Hundreds 9−8=1.
Thousands 7−5=2.
Answer: 2,185."
STUDENT B — Count Up
"Start at 5,847 and count UP to 8,032 in steps.
5,847 + 3 = 5,850 (+3)
5,850 + 150 = 6,000 (+150)
6,000 + 2,000 = 8,000 (+2,000)
8,000 + 32 = 8,032 (+32)
Total added: 3 + 150 + 2,000 + 32 = 2,185."
🧐

Same answer. Student A's algorithm works for any subtraction — even when regrouping is tricky. Student B's count-up is great when the numbers are close, or when subtracting feels harder than adding. Both are valid; both rely on place value.

Preview Lesson 4.02.03 — Other Ways to Add and Subtract

You've now learned the standard algorithm both ways — adding column by column with carries, and subtracting column by column with regrouping. That's the workhorse of multi-digit arithmetic.

Next lesson, we'll meet other strategies. Sometimes the standard algorithm isn't the fastest path. You'll learn mental math tricks like "compensate" (add an extra, then take it back), "decompose" (break a number apart), and "make a friendly number" (turn 199 into 200 first). The standard algorithm always works — but a thoughtful mathematician knows when to use a shortcut.
