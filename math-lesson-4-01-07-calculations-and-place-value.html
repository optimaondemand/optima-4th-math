Today's Mission
Put the whole unit to work. Use rounding to ESTIMATE a sum or difference, then add or subtract by place to get the EXACT answer. Compare the two to check that your answer makes sense.
❓
Essential Question
How can place value make calculations both faster AND more honest?

Every lesson in this unit used place value — to read numbers, to find patterns, to compare, to round. Today, place value pulls another job: doing calculations.

You'll estimate a sum by rounding both numbers first. Then you'll add them exactly, by place. The estimate tells you what to expect; the exact answer tells you the truth. If they don't agree, something's off — and you can find it.

📐 What You Just Saw

Estimate first. Compute exactly. Compare.

Step 1 — Estimate: round both numbers to a place you can add in your head. Add the rounded versions.

Step 2 — Compute exactly: stack the original numbers by place. Add or subtract one place at a time, starting from the ones. Carry when a place hits 10.

Step 3 — Compare: does the exact answer match the estimate? If yes, trust it. If no — something's wrong. Re-check.

This is what fluency looks like. You're not just answering — you're predicting, computing, and self-checking. Place value runs all three.

🔗 Three Estimates, One Truth

The exact sum of 487,290 + 312,580 is 799,870. Three different rounding choices give three different estimates — each closer or further from the truth.

Round to Estimate How close to 799,870?
nearest 1,000 487,000 + 313,000 = 800,000 Off by only 130 — very close.
nearest 10,000 490,000 + 310,000 = 800,000 Also off by 130 — coincidence.
nearest 100,000 500,000 + 300,000 = 800,000 Still 130 off — but coarser tool.

The pattern: Rounder estimates give faster math but coarser answers. The exact sum is the truth. The estimate is the check. Both together is fluency.

Worked Example: Find the Difference
Estimate, subtract by place, check.
Problem

The home team drew 564,830 fans last season. The away team drew 318,470. How many MORE fans did the home team draw? Use the estimate-then-check workflow.

Watch the Math Crew work through it:

F
Felix — estimate
"Round each to the nearest 10,000. 564,830 → 560,000 (decider thousands 4, round down). 318,470 → 320,000 (decider thousands 8, round up). Estimate: 560,000 − 320,000 = 240,000."
A
Atlas — subtract by place
"Stack them. Subtract place by place, starting from ones. Ones: 0 − 0 = 0. Tens: 3 − 7 — need to borrow. Take 1 from hundreds: 13 − 7 = 6. Hundreds is now 7. 7 − 4 = 3. Thousands: 4 − 8 — borrow again from ten-thousands. 14 − 8 = 6. Ten-thousands now 5. 5 − 1 = 4. Hundred-thousands: 5 − 3 = 2."

564,830 − 318,470 = 246,360

H
Hazel — draws the bar model
"I picture two bars: a long one (home) labeled 564,830, and a shorter one (away) labeled 318,470 below it. The DIFFERENCE is the part that sticks out — the gap between them."
C
Cora — checks against the estimate
"Felix's estimate said about 240,000. Atlas's exact answer is 246,360. Difference of about 6,360 — that's within the rounding error. The answer is REASONABLE. We can trust 246,360."
💡

The whole crew working together: Felix sets the target with a fast estimate. Atlas does the place-by-place subtraction. Hazel's bar model helps when the word problem is about comparison. Cora checks the exact answer against the estimate. If they don't roughly agree, something is off — and that's a flag to re-check the work.

Estimate, Then Exact — Same Story Told Twice
A word problem solved two ways.

Two students solved this word problem: "At a music festival, 246,800 fans came on Saturday and 184,300 came on Sunday. About how many in total?"

STUDENT A — Estimate Only
"The question says 'about how many.' I round both to nearest 10,000. 246,800 → 250,000. 184,300 → 180,000. Add: 250,000 + 180,000 = about 430,000. Done."
STUDENT B — Exact Then Compare
"I'll add them exactly: 246,800 + 184,300 = 431,100. Then I check against my own quick estimate (≈430,000). They agree — the answer is reasonable."
🧐

Both students get a correct, useful answer. Student A's estimate (430,000) is enough when the question says "about how many." Student B's exact answer (431,100) is needed when the question asks for precision. The word "about" tells you which one fits.

🔄 Find the decider digit for each round.
Nearest 100 → decider is tens (6). Nearest 1,000 → decider is hundreds (5). Nearest 10,000 → decider is thousands (4). Apply: 5 or higher rounds UP, 4 or lower rounds DOWN. Note: a decider of exactly 5 still rounds UP.
