Number Bonds

A picture of a number split into two parts. The whole goes in one circle, the parts in two others.

2-minute readGrades K–5Drawn in the textbook

What it is

One picture holds four facts: 5 + 3 = 8, 3 + 5 = 8, 8 − 5 = 3, 8 − 3 = 5.

That's why the bond comes before fact families. Students see that adding and subtracting are the same relationship read two ways.

Where it shows up

  • K–1: all the ways to make 5, then 10.
  • Making ten: 9 + 4 → split the 4 into 1 and 3. 9 + 1 = 10. 10 + 3 = 13.
  • Grades 2–5: 47 splits into 40 and 7. 1 splits into 3/4 and 1/4. Same picture, bigger numbers.

How to start it with students

  1. Objects first. Give 5 counters. Split them into two groups. How many ways can you do it?
  2. Then draw. Record each split as a bond.
  3. Then numbers. Write the matching number sentences.

How to use the tool

  1. Tap a circle and type a number.
  2. Type ? in the circle you need to find.
  3. The whole goes in the big circle. The parts go in the small ones.
  4. Check: do the parts add up to the whole?

Watch for

Whole in a part circle.Ask: which number is the biggest? That one is the whole.
"Bonds are for adding."Hide a part, not the whole. Now it's subtraction.
Only one answer.Ask for every way to make 6. Then: how do you know you found them all?
