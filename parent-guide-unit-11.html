This Unit at a Glance

Area is how many square units cover a flat shape. Your student finds the area of rectangles when the sides use different units, finds a missing side when the area is known, and then finds the area of shapes that aren't one simple rectangle. Perimeter, the distance around a shape, comes next, starting with lesson 11.04.

The big idea

Same unit first, then length × width. The area is in square units.
Know the area and one side? Divide, then multiply to check.
Not one rectangle? See the rectangles inside it.

The story

Your student helps Leonardo da Vinci, the painter and inventor, in his workshop in Milan. The Duke's gold is costly, so not one sheet can be wasted, and every job needs careful measuring. His helper Bernardo keeps making mistakes your student gets to fix. Each lesson, Leonardo also shows a keepsake: the Mona Lisa, a drawing of a flying machine, and his notebook, written backwards.

The Math Crew (Atlas, Felix, Hazel and Cora) shows up in each lesson with four ways to solve the opening problem. Your student picks the way most like their own and says why.

Lesson by Lesson

LessonWhat we learnAn example
11.01Area of rectangles, part 1Finding area when the sides use different units.
Cover a rectangle with square units, then multiply length × width. Put both sides in the same unit first: feet to inches, or yards to feet. A square needs only one measurement, because all 4 sides are equal. Different rectangles can have the same area, and a two-step word problem asks how many buckets of plaster cover a wall. Story: cover the Duke's panels in gold, without wasting a sheet.		1 ft 3 in × 4 in = 60 sq in
1 ft 3 in is 15 in, and 15 × 4 = 60. Or split at the foot: 12 × 4 = 48 and 3 × 4 = 12; 48 + 12 = 60.
11.02Area of rectangles, part 2Finding a missing side when you know the area.
Build rows of the side you know, or think "side × ? = area" and divide. Check by multiplying. For a square, ask what number times itself makes the area. If the side is in feet and the area is in square inches, change the side to inches first, even a fraction of a foot like 34 ft (split 12 inches into 4 equal parts). Story: the storeroom floor, and the Duke's letter asking for a panel of exactly 48 gold sheets, 4 inches wide.		91 sq in, 7 in wide → 13 in long
7 × ? = 91. Split 91: 70 ÷ 7 = 10 and 21 ÷ 7 = 3, so 13. Check: 13 × 7 = 91. It's 13 inches, not 13 square inches.
11.03Composite figuresFinding the area of a shape made of rectangles.
Two ways: split the shape into rectangles and add, or find the whole rectangle and take away the missing part. For a border or a path, add it on both sides first (2 + 8 + 2), then take away the middle. A side with no label is part of a longer side, so subtract to find it. Story: a gold border for the Duke's mirror, an L-shaped floor, a T-shaped table top, and a wall with a door and a window.		L-shaped rug = 64 sq ft
A 12 ft by 8 ft rectangle with an 8 ft by 4 ft corner missing: 96 − 32 = 64. Or split: 12 × 4 = 48 and 4 × 4 = 16; 48 + 16 = 64.

How We Teach It

Each lesson builds the idea in three steps: see it (lay tiles on a grid), picture it (label a drawing) and write it (the number sentence). Your student lays one row of tiles along the top and one column down the side. The row shows how many fit along the length, and the column shows how many rows there are. That's all they need to multiply.

1. Are the lengths in the same units? If not, convert to the smaller unit.
2. Multiply length × width.
3. Write the area in square units.

Leonardo's question. "Ask it every time: are the lengths in the same units?" These are Leonardo's Measuring Rules, and they come back on every page.

The same picture, run backward. In 11.02 the area is known and a side is missing. Your student lays rows of the side they know until the tiles run out. Then they think "side × ? = area," divide, and multiply to check. In 11.03 the shape isn't one rectangle, so they look for the rectangles inside it and pick the way with easier steps.

Mix-Ups to Watch For

Bernardo's mistakes
  • Multiplying feet by inches. A lid 1 ft long and 2 in wide needs 12 × 2 = 24 one-inch sheets, not 1 × 2 = 2. Ask: "Are the lengths in the same units?"
  • Adding the sides, or going around the edge. Area multiplies. Adding the sides, or adding all four, finds part of the distance around, not what covers the inside.
  • Multiplying when they should divide. An area of 35 square inches with one side of 5 in has another side of 35 ÷ 5 = 7 in, not 35 × 5. Ask: "5 times what makes 35?"
  • Square units on a side. A missing side is a length: 13 inches, not 13 square inches.
  • A border on one side only. A border goes on both ends. A 3 in by 2 in card with a 1-inch border is 1 + 3 + 1 = 5 in long and 4 in wide all together.
  • Leaving the last bit uncovered. If one bucket covers 10 square feet, 37 square feet needs 4 buckets. 3 buckets cover only 30.

Try It at Home

  1. Tile count. Find a tiled floor, a backsplash or a sheet of square stickers. Count the tiles in one row and in one column, multiply, then count to check.
  2. Same area, different shape. With 12 square crackers or sticky notes, build every rectangle that uses all 12 (no holes, straight sides). Is a long, thin one really bigger?
  3. The missing side. Lay squares in a rectangle while your student looks away. Say how many squares there are and how many are in one row. Your student finds the number of rows, then checks by multiplying.
  4. Mixed-unit measuring. Measure a placemat or a book cover with a ruler. If one side is more than 12 inches, write it in feet and inches, then change it back to inches before finding the area.
  5. Floor plan. Sketch an L-shaped room, a hallway or a garden bed. Find its area by splitting it into rectangles, then again by taking away the missing corner. Do both ways match?
  6. Picture frame. Measure a framed photo, outside and inside. How many square inches is the frame itself?

The best question to ask at home is Leonardo's: "Are the lengths in the same units?"
